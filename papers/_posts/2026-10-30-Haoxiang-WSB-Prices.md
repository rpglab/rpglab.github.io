# Abstract
This paper asks whether sequential wind, solar, and battery storage additions that eliminate unserved energy also remove modeled reserve scarcity and transmission congestion. Thirty-one cumulative portfolio states are evaluated on a modified IEEE 24-bus system for a constructed stressed day using a 24-hour day-ahead unit commitment model with network constraints, co-optimized energy and upward reserve, chronological battery storage, and an approximation of the Electric Reliability Council of Texas operating reserve demand curve. The thermal baseline has 106 MWh of unserved energy, whereas 450 MW of wind is sufficient to serve all demand. However, with 1,500 MW of wind, the hourly modeled reserve scarcity price still reaches $5,000/MWh because wind output declines after the system peak. Adding 1,800 MW of solar reduces the load weighted daily average modeled reserve scarcity price from $1,136.2/MWh to $101.2/MWh and confines modeled reserve scarcity to three evening hours. Adding 250 MW/1,000 MWh of battery storage further lowers the average to $4.3/MWh and reduces wind curtailment from 631 MWh to 306 MWh. Transmission congestion nevertheless remains: all residual wind curtailment occurs at bus 21, wind capture prices at buses 21 and 23 differ by $31.8/MWh, and identical battery storage plants yield normalized net energy market revenues of $201/(MW·day) and $121/(MW·day) at buses 19 and 21, respectively. These results show that unserved energy, modeled reserve scarcity, and locational congestion effects measure distinct operating conditions and should be evaluated separately but interpreted together.
